# P1 · 3D map of the boxwork box (terrain, route rehearsal)

Owner: Juan (`@JuanCorredor2023`) · Issue: #42 · PRs: #54 (block 1 of 6), #63 (block 2), #66 (block 3), #67 (block 4), #69 (block 5), this PR (block 6, closing) · Status: done (terrain; route, stations and habitat on the terrain; route rehearsal at eye height; Sun of Mars by sol and local time; shadows cast by the relief; a shareable view and picture; regression pass and page README). Rover photos in the 3D view are a separate piece (P3) · Last update: 2026-11-14

## Question
What does the astronaut gain from seeing the planned EVA in 3D before leaving the habitat: the relief of the 2 × 2 km box, the planned route on the ground and a walk-through at eye height? The note follows that order. The first part is how to show a 475 m hillside whose features of interest are 1–2 m high, in a browser, without overselling what the DEM contains.

## Data
- Base grid (JB, `base-grid-v2`): `dem_1m.tif` (2000 × 2000, 1 m) [Parker2016] and `ortho_color_025.tif` (8000 × 8000, 0.25 m) [HiRISE-Gale]. No cell is NaN in the box.
- Nothing is filtered out. The orthophoto is only resized (low preset) or cropped (high preset).

## Method

### Assets for the browser (`src/viz3d/build_terrain_assets.py` → `data/tiles/3d/`)
The output lives inside `data/tiles/` because the API already serves that folder at `/tiles/`: the 3D page needs no change in `api/`. It is **not** part of the `tiles-v2` release (see Reproduce).

| File | Content |
|---|---|
| `height_2m.bin` | DEM, mean of each 2 × 2 m block, 1000 × 1000 uint16 (step 7.2 mm) |
| `height_low_2m.bin` | "hillside": DEM smoothed with a Gaussian of σ = 25 m, same grid |
| `detail_2m.bin` | "local relief": Gaussian σ = 3 m of (DEM − hillside), same grid |
| `normal_detail.png` | tangent-space normal map of the local relief, 1000 × 1000 |
| `ortho_4096.jpg` | orthophoto of the whole box at 4096 px (0.49 m/px), low preset |
| `ortho_hi_<r><c>.jpg` | four 4096 px tiles at the native 0.25 m, overlapping 48 m, high preset |
| `terrain.json` | grid, encodings and the numbers quoted below |

- **Heights as numbers, not as an image.** A raw uint16 file is read exactly with one `fetch`; a height PNG would have to hide the value in colour channels, which browsers may alter (colour management, premultiplied alpha).
- **Two presets from the same files.** High: 2 m mesh, 2.0 M triangles, photo at 0.25 m. Low: the 2 m grid averaged to 4 m in the browser, 0.5 M triangles, one 4096 px photo.

### Exaggerating only the local relief
The box is a hillside: elevations span 475 m (−3876 to −3401 m) while knolls and scarps are metres high. A uniform vertical exaggeration would turn the hillside into a wall. The page draws

`shown = height + (k − 1) · detail`

so k = 1 is the DEM itself and k > 1 amplifies only the smoothed local relief. Whenever k ≠ 1 the screen says "Local relief ×k (exaggerated)".

**Why the local relief is smoothed, and why the slider stops at ×5.** At 1 m the DEM minus the hillside is dominated by a pebbly texture and by mosaic seams, not by landforms ([p1_fig1](../figures/p1_fig1_relief_exaggeration.jpg), top row). This agrees with the base grid note: the effective DEM resolution is coarser than 1 m. Exaggerating it exaggerates noise. A 3 m Gaussian keeps knolls, scarps and the lineated outcrop and removes most of the texture (bottom row). Even so, at ×10 half of the box would be shaded as steeper than 39°, so the page offers at most ×5 (`max_exaggeration` in `terrain.json`). Because the exaggeration term uses the smoothed field, the noise itself is never amplified: it stays at ×1 in the geometry.

### Shading: hillside normals plus a normal map
Vertex normals come from the hillside, and the local relief is shaded by a normal map with strength k. Consequences:
- Both presets shade the same, because the normal map does not depend on the mesh spacing.
- Moving the slider only rewrites vertex heights; no normals are recomputed.
- Slopes are stored multiplied by a gain of 4 so that small slopes survive 8 bits (decoding error < 0.02 in slope, test `test_normal_map_decodes_back_to_the_slopes`).

**What the shading shows.** Hillside, knolls and scarps. It does **not** show the boxwork ridges: they are 1–3 m wide and the DEM does not resolve them. In the 3D view the boxwork network is recognised by the HiRISE photo draped on the terrain. The page says so on screen.

### Page (`web/3d/`, three.js r187 [threejs], WebGL)
- A separate page at `/3d/`, served by the existing static mount; three.js is vendored (`web/3d/vendor/`, 8 files, MIT) so the page needs no internet.
- three.js instead of deck.gl's `TerrainLayer` inside the HUD: shadows and terrain draping are experimental in deck.gl 9.1, live exaggeration reloads the mesh there, and a separate page does not collide with the HUD work.
- Scene axes: X east, Y up, Z south, origin at the box centre. CRS coordinates (about 8.1 × 10⁶ m) are shifted in JavaScript (64-bit) before reaching the GPU (32-bit).
- No custom shaders: `MeshStandardMaterial` with the photo and the normal map. In the high preset the four photo tiles share one UV set through per-texture transforms.
- Rendering on demand (only when the camera moves), a compass, and a readout of CRS position and elevation.

### Camera: an arcball on the terrain (`web/3d/controls.js`, `web/3d/camera_math.js`)
three.js' stock map controls orbit around a target that drifts away from what the user is looking at, so they were replaced. Every gesture acts on the **terrain point under the cursor**, found by casting a ray against the height grid (step proportional to the height above ground, then bisection):
- **Rotate** (drag, or one finger): the view turns around that point, which stays fixed on screen. The camera has a heading and a tilt but never rolls, so the horizon stays level. A ring marks the point while rotating.
- **Move** (right-drag, Shift + drag, or two fingers): the grabbed point follows the cursor and the camera keeps its altitude.
- **Zoom** (wheel or pinch): towards that point, which stays under the cursor; two-finger twist turns the heading.
- If the cursor is off the terrain, rotation and zoom use the terrain at the centre of the screen (or the box centre), never a point in empty space. The camera stays at least 2 m above the ground. `?drag=pan` swaps the two mouse drags (map style).

### Route, stations and habitat on the terrain (`web/3d/plan_model.js`, `api.js`, `overlays.js`, `markers.js`)
Rule: the page draws what the plan answer contains, as is. It does not plan and does not assume where the route came from; every optional field (profile, turn, goal, totals) may be missing.

- **Where the plan comes from.** The page only reads: `GET /plan/last`, the last plan made in the 2D planner (the server answers `{request, plan}`; the page uses `plan`). **It never plans by itself.** The reason: the server saves every plan it makes as the last plan, and the guide screen on the arm tablet loads that one, so a page that planned on opening would silently replace the astronaut's plan. With no saved plan the page shows the terrain alone and says "No plan yet: plan an EVA in the 2D planner, then press Reload plan". A second button, "Plan with default settings" (or `?plan=default` in the address), sends the 2D planner's own default request when the user asks for it, and says that the result becomes the last plan: the saved stations scored by the rubric, 1000 Wh with a 10 % margin, 120 min, cells over 30° blocked, start at the box centre. If a request fails the terrain still works and the panel gives the server's reason. The panel shows the source, length, time, energy, the answer's warnings and a "Reload plan" button.
- **Plan model (no three.js, tested in Node).** The GeoJSON becomes routes, points and an optional per-vertex profile; a profile array is kept only if it has one value per vertex, and a bad field is listed as ignored instead of breaking the page. Each route is split so that no step is longer than 1 m; every new vertex keeps its fractional index into the original vertices and its cumulative 2D length, which is what the rehearsal needs to interpolate the profile and walk the route by time or by distance. The route is cut into outbound and return at `turn_index` when the answer has one.
- **Line draped on the mesh.** The route is drawn with fat lines (`Line2`, 4 px wide at any distance). Besides its own vertices the line gets a vertex wherever it crosses an edge of a terrain triangle (mesh column, row or cell diagonal), and every vertex is placed on the drawn triangles (`Terrain.surfaceAt`) plus 0.3 m. Between two consecutive vertices the ground is a plane, so the whole line, not only its vertices, is 0.3 m above the ground. Heights are recomputed when the relief slider moves.
- **Two legs, two passes.** Outbound in the 2D planner's cyan; the return leg in pink and shifted 0.8 m to the right of the direction of travel (direction taken over ±3 m, shift fading to zero over 4 m at both ends), because an E4 round trip returns along almost the same cells and one leg would hide the other. Each leg is drawn twice: solid with the depth test, and at 30 % opacity without it, so the route can be followed behind a knoll. The 1 m segments overlap on screen, so the faint pass uses the stencil buffer to paint each pixel once; without it the "faint" line adds up to a solid one (numbers in Checks).
- **Markers.** A generic layer takes `{id, x, y, kind, label, color, data}` in CRS metres and draws a pin (label, thin pole, ground dot) as a sprite of constant size on screen, never hidden by the terrain, like a map label. Stations take the 2D planner's colours by type (type from the answer, else from `GET /stations`, else grey); the station the route goes to is highlighted (filled label, taller pole); a station that a tour skipped (`visited: false`) is dimmed. The E5 tour also writes a `stop` point on the route for every station it visits; it gets no pin of its own when it names a station of the plan (block 6: before, each visited station had two pins). The habitat is also a white dome 8 m across standing on the lowest ground under its rim. `pick()` returns the marker under a screen point using the painted pixels of the pin, and a click calls `onSelect` (hook for the station card, and for rover photos in P3).
- **Relief slider** from ×1 to ×5 in steps of 0.5; the page now opens at ×2 (`?k=` changes it). Terrain, route, markers and the camera's floor follow it, at most once per frame.

### Rehearsing the route (`web/3d/rehearse_model.js`, `rehearse.js`, `card.js`)
"Rehearse the route" walks the planned route in first person before the EVA; "Exit" gives the free-flight view back as it was. The page still plans nothing: it plays the profile of the plan answer.

- **Time base.** The clock is the profile's `t_s` when the answer has one, so the walker moves at the planner's own pace (0.52 to 0.91 m/s on the E4 route). A stop is charged to the step that arrives at it: in the E4 answer `t_s` jumps 601.6 s over the last metre before the goal, which is 1.7 s of walking plus the 10 min of service. The model splits any step that is more than 5 times slower than the median pace, and more than 10 s longer than that, into a walk at the pace of the step before and a wait at its end; during the wait the walker stands on the vertex, energy and O₂ keep rising with the clock and the energy and time to return do not change. With no profile the time is distance at an assumed 1.0 m/s, and the screen says "assumed 1 m/s".
- **Profile values come from the true route**, linearly between its vertices, so at a vertex they are the answer's own numbers. The energy bar (used / needed to return / free of the usable budget) and its two lines of text repeat the arithmetic and the wording of the 2D planner's route profile.
- **Smoothed camera path, and why.** The planner moves cell to cell in eight directions, so the true path is a staircase that turns 45° at a vertex every metre or two: a camera walked along it shakes. The camera follows a copy of the route averaged along the path with a Gaussian of σ = 2.5 m (sampled every 0.5 m), pinned exactly at the route ends, at the stops and at the turn (the path is continued by its own mirror image through those points, so they do not move), and smoothed less wherever it would be more than 1.2 m from the true route, so it never cuts a corner by more than 1.5 m. Only the camera position and heading use this copy.
- **Heading** is the direction to the point 12 m further along the camera path (the 2D planner's heading arrow looks 12 vertices ahead), never beyond the next stop: looking past the point where the route turns back would spin the view. It is held over the last 3 m before a stop, and while the walker stands there it turns from the arrival to the departure direction. While playing, the view follows a change of heading with a time constant of 1/6 s.
- **Stops.** A stop is a vertex where the walker stands still, a vertex the answer lists (`stop_index` / `visit_order` of a tour), or the vertex nearest to a station that the answer marks as visited or as the goal, when it is within 3 m. Nothing assumes a single goal. Arriving at a stop while playing pauses and shows the station card (id, type label from `GET /stations/rubric`, score, service time, arrival time); play again runs the wait at the playback speed with the walker standing.
- **Camera.** Eye 1.7 m above the shown ground averaged over ±3 m of path (so it does not bob over every 2 m facet), never under 1.6 m above the ground right under it; vertical field of view 60°; near plane 0.3 m instead of the 2 m of free flight. Dragging looks around (offsets from the direction of travel, ±70° of pitch); double-click or "Recentre" looks forward. "Chase view" puts the camera 15 m behind the walker and 8 m above the ground it stands on, at least 2 m above the ground under the camera, with a 1.7 m marker for the walker.
- **True scale is forced, and why.** While rehearsing, the relief slider is locked at ×1 and says so; the previous value comes back on exit. The exaggeration is a reading aid from above. At eye height it would put a wall where there is a knoll, and the slopes the astronaut will walk are what the rehearsal is for.
- **Player.** Play / pause (also the space bar), ×1, ×30, ×60 (default) and ×180, a scrubber over the whole plan time, and "time of total · outbound or returning · metres". Frames are drawn only while playing, dragging or easing.
- **Walkback lines on pause.** As in the 2D planner, pausing asks `POST /walkback` for the 30 min return line (red) and the energy-safe boundary (cyan) from the walker's position with the energy left (usable − used, rounded as the 2D planner does), with the plan's own slope limit and habitat; the lines are draped on the mesh like the route and removed on play or exit. This request computes and answers only: `api/main.py` `walkback()` writes no file.
- **`flyTo`** (`controls.js`): an eased flight of about 1 s to a pose given as CRS position, eye height above the ground, heading, pitch and field of view. Clicking a pin in free flight shows the same card with "Fly here", which views the station from 60 m. A pose may be lower than 2 m and may look above the horizon (rover photo poses, P3); the next gesture brings the free-flight limits back.

### The Sun of Mars (`web/3d/mars_sun.js`, `sun_model.js`, `sun.js`)
The scene is lit from where the Sun is at the box centre at a Curiosity sol and a local time, so the astronaut sees which slopes will be lit or dark during the EVA. Until block 4 the light was fixed (from the west, 35° high).

- **Algorithm.** Mars24 [Allison2000], written from the equation list of the GISS page (steps A-1 to D-6): days since J2000 in Terrestrial Time, mean anomaly, fictitious mean Sun, seven planetary perturbation terms, equation of centre, areocentric solar longitude Ls, equation of time, Coordinated Mars Time, local mean and local true solar time, subsolar point, solar declination, and from them the zenith angle and azimuth at a site. The module has no dependencies and is tested in Node. It exists only in JavaScript: the figure and the numbers of this note are made by running the same file with Node (`src/viz3d/sun_table.mjs`), not by a second implementation.
- **Conventions, and the traps.** (1) The page is west-positive; the module takes longitudes **east**, as the CRS gives them (longitude = x / R, latitude = y / R on the sphere of R = 3,396,190 m), and converts inside. (2) TT − UTC is a parameter: by default 32.184 s plus the leap seconds in force at the date (table from 1999; 64.184 s for the worked examples of 2000 and 2004, 69.184 s since 2017). (3) Mars24 latitudes are planetographic and the CRS ones planetocentric: the site latitude is converted (−4.799° becomes −4.856°, flattening from radii 3396.19 and 3376.20 km). (4) Azimuth is the compass bearing of the Sun, from north through east; the unit vector towards the Sun in the scene (X east, Y up, Z south) is (sin A cos e, sin e, −cos A cos e). (5) Curiosity's mission clock is the local mean solar time at 137.42° E, the planned landing site, with sol 0 the sol of landing (Mars Sol Date 49269); the box centre is at 137.386° E.
- **The clock of the page is local true solar time at the box centre** (12:00 is the Sun on the meridian), because that is what tells where the light comes from. The sol is counted on the same clock (`trueSolarDate`: Mars Sol Date + longitude / 360 + equation of time / 360 − 49269), so the time slider has no jump at any hour. This sol number is the mission's except within the equation of time of midnight: on sol 4711 the equation of time is +37.5 min, so 15:00 here is 14:23 on the mission clock, and the first 37 min after true midnight still belong to mission sol 4710. The inverse (sol and local true solar time to UTC) is solved by Newton steps on that count.
- **In the page.** A "Sun" section in the panel: sol (number), local solar time (slider over the 24 h in 5 min steps) and a readout in words, e.g. "Sun 44° above the horizon · light from the west (azimuth 282°) · Ls 167°", plus the Earth date. Default: **sol 4711 at 15:00**; `?sol=4711&lt=15:00` in the address changes it. `?light=az,el` remains as a debug override that fixes the light as before and switches the Sun off.
- **Light levels are a look, not a radiometric model.** The directional light keeps its previous strength (2.6) with the Sun above 12° and fades smoothly to zero at the horizon (the planet itself casts no shadow in the scene, so a Sun under the horizon must light nothing). The sky (hemisphere) light goes from 1.1 by day to 0.3 at night between +6° and −6°, never black, and the sky colour darkens with it; below the horizon the panel says "Night: the Sun is below the horizon". No atmosphere, dust opacity, twilight or sky brightness is computed.
- **While rehearsing the route** the Sun advances with the EVA clock from the chosen start (UTC of the start + elapsed seconds of plan time); the panel shows the current local time and its controls are locked. Leaving the rehearsal goes back to the start time.
- **"Relief only"** hides the photo and shows plain grey ground, to judge the light without the shadows that the photo already carries; unticking it puts back the same textures and colours (one in the low preset, four in the high one).
- **Not modelled:** the horizon beyond the box (a hill outside it between the site and the Sun), refraction, the size of the solar disc. Shadows cast inside the box are the next subsection. One Sun direction is used for the whole 2 km box (its edges are 0.017° of longitude from the centre, 4 s of time).

### Cast shadows (`web/3d/shadow_fit.js`, `shadows.js`)
The sun light casts shadows with three.js' own shadow maps (r187, no custom shader): the scene's depth seen from the Sun is drawn into a texture and every lit pixel of the terrain is compared with it. The terrain casts and receives; the habitat dome casts; pins and route lines do neither. The agreed reference for the direction is this and no more: HiRISE takes its images at about 15:00 local time, so at 15:00 the shadows of the 3D map must fall towards the east, as in the photo.

- **The camera of the shadow map covers the whole block.** It is orthographic and looks along the sunlight. Its box is the bounding box of the block seen from the Sun (2 × 2 km, from the lowest to the highest shown height plus 5 m for the dome) with 4 m of margin across and 10 m in depth, computed by a function with no three.js (`fitShadowBox`, tested in Node). At 15:00 the box is 2387 × 2004 m; at 07:00 and 17:00 it is 2234 × 1026 m.
- **Map size and what one texel covers.** 4096 × 4096 texels in the high preset, 2048 × 2048 in the low one. Across the Sun's azimuth a texel covers 0.55 to 0.58 m of ground (high) or 1.09 to 1.17 m (low). Along the azimuth it is stretched by 1 / sin(elevation): 0.71 m at 15:00 and 1.01 m at 07:00 and 17:00 (high), 1.41 m and 2.01 m (low). The filter is three.js' PCF (five taps within one texel), so a shadow edge is about two texels soft.
- **Drawn on demand, not every frame** (`renderer.shadowMap.autoUpdate = false`). The map is drawn again when the Sun has moved more than 0.2° since it was drawn (a 100 m shadow moves 0.35 m, under one texel), when the relief slider changes the heights, when the plan changes (the dome), and when the switch is turned on. Between redraws the old map is used with the camera it was drawn with. Below the horizon nothing is drawn: the sun light is zero there.
- **Why sunlit ground shows no acne.** The depth map holds the faces turned away from the Sun (three.js' default for a one-sided material). A point in shadow on a height field always has such a face between it and the Sun, and sunlit ground is never compared with itself, which is the usual cause of acne. The remaining guard is a depth bias of 1.5 texels (0.82 to 0.87 m along the light in the high preset, 1.63 to 1.75 m in the low one): ground closer than that to a point does not shade it. `normalBias` stays 0. The dome casts with its sunlit side instead, so the bias leaves no gap behind it.
- **Switch.** A "Shadows" checkbox in the Sun section, on by default in both presets; `?shadows=0` starts without them. Off is the page of block 4: a light that casts no shadow compiles the same programs as before.
- **The shadows are those of the relief as shown.** They are true only at relief ×1; at ×2 to ×5 they are the shadows of the exaggerated surface. They show knolls and scarps, not the 1–2 m boxwork ridges, which the DEM does not resolve. The page says so in "About this view".

### A view to share: address and picture (`web/3d/view_link.js`, `main.js`)
- **`?view=x,y,z,heading,pitch`** places the free-flight camera: CRS metres, the camera's elevation in metres on the DEM datum, heading from north and pitch below the horizon in degrees. The position is absolute, not a height above the ground, because the shown ground moves with the relief slider and the camera does not. Written to the centimetre and the thousandth of a degree; the opening view is `view=8143537.49,-286459.66,-2146.25,0,37`. It is applied after the relief is set, through the same limits as the gestures (2 m above the shown ground, pitch 2° to 89.5°).
- **"Copy link to this view"** (About section) builds the page's own address with the view and what changes the picture: `k`, `sol`, `lt` and `shadows=0` when they are off. `check`, `spin` and `plan` are never carried: a link with `plan=default` would make the server replace the saved plan. The link is copied to the clipboard where the browser allows it (localhost or https) and always shown in a box, since the page is also opened from other machines of the local network. It is not offered while rehearsing (the link is of the free-flight view).
- **"Save image"** draws the frame again and saves the canvas as a PNG through the browser's download (no server): terrain, route and pins at the canvas' size, without the panels.
- **Page README** (`web/3d/README.md`): how to run it, controls, every address parameter and check, the files and the known limits, for teammates.

## Checks
- **Assets against the DEM** (`tests/viz3d/test_terrain_assets.py`, 10 tests): stored heights equal the 2 × 2 m block means of the DEM within 2 cm everywhere; the north-west corner is the lowest and the south-east the highest, as in the DEM; slopes and normal map follow east and north with row 0 = north; a plane is reproduced with zero mesh error.

## Limitations
- The DEM does not resolve the boxwork ridges; neither the geometry nor the shading shows them. Only the photo does.
- The local relief is a smoothed field: at k > 1 small real features below about 6 m across are not exaggerated, and what is exaggerated is no longer to scale. Shading is exaggerated with it.
- The photo carries its own illumination (HiRISE images are taken in the mid-afternoon); the synthetic light is added on top.
- **The light levels are a look.** There is no atmosphere or dust in the model: no opacity, no sky brightness, no twilight colours, no reddening at a low Sun. The fade of the sun light under 12° and the dimming of the sky around the horizon are chosen by eye; brightness on screen is not irradiance.
- **Only the box casts shadows** and the horizon is that of a smooth planet. Ground that terrain outside the 2 × 2 km box would shade is drawn lit, which matters most near its edges at 07:00 and 17:00, and sunrise and sunset do not account for the terrain around the box (the hillside keeps rising beyond its south-east corner).
- **A shadow texel is as large as the ridges.** One texel of the shadow map covers 0.55 to 1.0 m of ground in the high preset and 1.1 to 2.0 m in the low one, and the mesh under it has 2 m or 4 m cells from a DEM that does not resolve the 1–2 m boxwork ridges. The shadows are those of knolls and scarps. A shadow thinner than about two texels fades out, so the render shades slightly less ground than the computation (21.2 % against 22.9 % at 07:00), and ground closer than the bias (0.8 m high preset, 1.7 m low) to what would shade it stays lit.
- **Shadows follow the relief as shown.** At ×2 to ×5 they are the shadows of the exaggerated surface, far larger than the real ones (at 15:00, 6.2 % of the block at ×5 against 0.08 % at ×1). Only ×1 is true, and the page opens at ×2.
- **Shadows appear twice with the photo on.** The photo already carries the shadows of its own mid-afternoon Sun, including those of ridges and boulders that the DEM does not have; the computed ones are drawn over it. "Relief only" shows the computed ones alone.
- **The 15:00 direction rests on little shaded ground at true scale.** At ×1 the DEM shades 0.08 % of the block at 15:00: 17 pixels of the check (one in the low preset), plus the dome's shadow. The east direction has more ground to stand on at ×5 (3156 pixels, 98.9 % of them also shaded by the computation with the true Sun) and at 17:00. The rendered shadows were not compared with the shadows seen in the HiRISE photo or with any catalogue.
- **Shadows are hard and binary in the model.** No penumbra from the solar disc, no light scattered into shadows beyond the constant sky light, and the dome receives no shadow. The independent calculation of the check shares the mesh and the bias rule with the render (it is another algorithm, not other data) and samples its ray every half mesh cell.
- **Cost was measured on one laptop.** By arithmetic, not measured, the 4096 map takes about 130 MB of GPU memory (depth plus the colour target three.js attaches) and the 2048 one about 32 MB; whether a tablet holds 30 fps with shadows was not measured, so "on by default in the low preset" rests on this laptop.
- **Two lights at once.** The photo keeps the shadows of its own mid-afternoon Sun under the computed one, so with the photo on a morning view shows both. "Relief only" is the honest view of the computed light and of the computed shadows.
- **Shading is exaggerated with the relief.** The normal map is scaled by the relief factor, so at the default ×2 slopes look lit and dark as if they were twice as steep; the east-west contrast measured above is for ×2. At true scale (and always while rehearsing) it is the DEM's own.
- The Sun's position was checked against the two worked examples of the Mars24 page and by its own geometry (meridian at noon, east and west), not against the Mars24 program or SPICE for sol 4711. The mission's clock is 2.9 s behind the one computed here, cause not established. Leap seconds are a table up to 2017: one added later would shift everything by 1 s until the table is updated.
- Planetocentric and planetographic latitude are not mixed: the CRS latitude is converted before use. The difference is 0.057° at the box; "up" in the scene is the DEM's vertical, and the two verticals differ by that same angle, which is ignored.
- Close to midnight the Sun passes less than 1° from straight down at this site and season, so its azimuth there swings by 180° within minutes; it lights nothing then.
- DEM mosaic seams are visible as straight lines in the raw local relief (fig. 1, top row); the smoothing attenuates them but does not remove them.
- At eye height the ground looks blurred: the photo has 0.25 m pixels and the eye is 1.7 m from it, and the 2 m facets of the mesh show. The rehearsal gives the lie of the land (slopes, horizon, where the route goes), not the surface underfoot; real Curiosity images (P3) address that.
- The walker's position is simulated: it is the plan played back on its own clock, not a position of the astronaut. With no profile even the pace is an assumption (1 m/s).
- The camera does not walk the planner's cells: its path is up to 0.8 m (E4 route) or 1.1 m (fixed route) from the true route, and nothing checks that the smoothed path avoids a blocked cell the route went around. Times, distances and energy are those of the true route.
- A stop's waiting time is inferred from the profile (a step far slower than its neighbours); a real step that slow would be shown as a short walk and a wait. At a stop with no waiting time the heading changes at once and only the on-screen easing softens it.
- The fixed route was tested only with its first 400 vertices: getting the whole route needs a `POST /plan`, which would replace the saved plan.
- The walkback lines come from `POST /walkback`, whose return energy differed by 2 % from the plan's own `rv_wh` at the point tested; the first request for a habitat takes several seconds. The radiation dose that the 2D profile text adds is not shown here.
- With the near plane at 0.3 m the depth buffer is coarser far away; whether the route line flickers against the ground at long range from eye height was not measured.
- The look of the first-person and chase views was judged from numbers (sky fraction, marker pixel) and one small capture, not from a careful visual review. Frames per second are from one laptop in an emulated viewport.
- After a `flyTo` pose that looks above the horizon, the first gesture tilts the view down to the free-flight limit (2°) and lifts the camera to 2 m.
- Performance is measured on one laptop; no tablet was available.
- The route shown is the planner's path on the 1 m grid, drawn as is: its steps in eight directions look like a zigzag from close up. The 0.8 m shift of the return leg is a drawing aid, not where the astronaut would walk, and beyond roughly 700 m (in a view 800 px high) it is under one pixel, so the two legs merge.
- Pins have the same size at any distance and are never hidden by the terrain: they give no cue of distance, and labels of stations a few metres apart (R0 and H0 are 10 m apart) overlap until the camera is close.
- "Plan with default settings" makes the server save its answer as the last plan (`data/plans/last_plan.json`): the guide screen will load it, and the next visit shows it as "last plan from the planner". The button says so, and the page never does it unasked.
- The multi-station tour was tested with one real answer, made in-process from the E5 PR branch at commit 474b4fd, not through a running server and not with other limits or station sets. E5 is not merged: if its answer changes shape before it is (the `stop` points, `stop_index`, `visit_order`), the fixture has to be made again. A stop's arrival time is inferred from the profile and differs by up to 0.5 s from the one E5 writes, which the page does not read. The default request sends all 13 saved stations, which this version of E5 accepts; a solver with a lower station limit would refuse it, and the page then shows the reason and the terrain only.
- The legs of a tour are drawn as "outbound" up to the last station and "return" from there, the turn the answer gives; the order of the visits is in the cards, not on the line.
- A view link carries the camera, relief, sol, time and the shadows switch, not "Relief only" nor the plan: the same address shows another route after the saved plan changes. The saved image is the canvas alone, at screen resolution.
- The regression pass used synthetic events in one browser (Chromium) on one laptop; no real touch screen was used, and `?plan=default` was not opened.

## What other pieces can use

- `web/3d/rehearse_model.js` (no dependencies): the state of a walker at any time of a plan (position, heading, stops, interpolated profile) and the split of a stop into walk and wait (guide screen, Sebas). `web/3d/card.js`: `showCard({title, subtitle, rows, note, media, actions})` and `controls.flyTo({x, y, eyeHeight, heading, pitch, fov})` for rover photo poses (P3).
- `web/3d/shadow_fit.js` (no dependencies): the orthographic box that holds a terrain block seen from a given Sun direction, and the ground size of a shadow texel. - `tests/viz3d/fixtures/plan_tour_e5.json` and `src/viz3d/make_tour_fixture.py`: a real `/plan` answer of the E5 tour and the way to make another without a server and without replacing the saved plan (HUD and guide tests, Sebas; E5, Diana).

## Reproduce

```
python -m pytest tests/viz3d -q
node --test tests/viz3d/                      # camera maths, plan model, rehearsal model, Mars time and Sun, shadow camera, view link (Node 26; nothing to install)

```
